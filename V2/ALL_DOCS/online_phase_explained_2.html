1. The Two-Energy Bookkeeping — the Exact Identity

The single most under-explained thing in the code is that there are two distinct energy numbers, they are supposed to diverge, and the divergence is exactly the freed energy. Part I named them; here is the algebra that makes them precise. All of this lives in controller.py (energy_pool, total_energy) and is surfaced by simulator.py (wcet_energy vs actual_energy).

Three lines that move energy

# __init__
energy_pool = B - total_energy()                  # = B - E0   (E0 = committed WCET energy)
# on_completion, step 1
energy_pool += observed_de                          # a job finished cheap; bank the saving
# on_completion, step 5  (spent = sum of committed a_e)
energy_pool -= spent                                # pay for the new downstream segments

Each committed decision raises seg_k/freq_idx, and total_energy() rises by exactly that decision's a_e (because a_e = energy_val(cum[k′],fz′) − energy_val(cum[k_off],fz_off) is the same quantity total_energy sums). So after a whole run with cumulative freed Σδe and cumulative spend Σae:

Enow = E0 + Σae   (WCET commitment = wcet_energy)
pool = (B − E0) + Σδe − Σae
⟹   pool + Enow = B + Σδe   and   actual_energy = B − pool = Enow − Σδe

The commit guard enforces pool − spent ≥ −1e-6 at every step, i.e. Σae ≤ (B − E0) + Σδe. Substituting:

Energy theorem. actual_energy ≤ B always (this is C3, and it is hard-guaranteed), while wcet_energy = Enow may exceed B by up to Σδe — the total energy other jobs actually saved. The "over-commitment" you see in wcet_energy > B is a bookkeeping artifact of pretending the early-finishers ran to WCET; it can never co-occur with the savings that funded it.
Reading the simulator output: Energy (WCET commit) can print a value above the budget and that is not a violation. The line that must stay ≤ B is Energy (realised) = actual_energy, and the feasibility verdict uses energy_pool ≥ −1e-6, which is the same condition.

2. Two Different Safety Proofs: Time vs Energy

Time and energy are both guaranteed, but by opposite mechanisms. This asymmetry is the heart of why the controller code is shaped the way it is.

Energy — "spend only what you banked"

Energy is global and accumulative. The pool only ever rises by realised savings (observed_de) and falls by committed spend. Because the guard checks the live pool before each commit, you can never spend energy you have not already observed being freed. Safety is retrospective: it is funded by the past.

Time — "assume the future is worst-case"

Time is local and perishable. _timing_ok_dynamic credits the reduced _eff only for jobs already in eff_override (i.e. already completed). Every not-yet-run job is charged its full committed effective time. Safety is conservative: it never bets on a future early finish.

Time theorem (what "provably safe" really means). The controller comment says additions are "provably safe." Precisely: the offline schedule satisfied every DBF window. _timing_ok_dynamic re-evaluates every window with current seg_k after applying a candidate; an addition is committed iff all windows still hold. So the a-priori window argument is not what guarantees safety — the explicit re-check plus trim is. The window argument only explains why early completion creates room (a completer's window loses observed_dt of demand, so something else can take it). No deadline miss is ever possible because an infeasible candidate is reverted, not committed.

Net: energy can be over-committed on paper but never over-spent in reality; time is never over-committed even on paper. The two ≥ −1e-6 / ≤ |window| checks in _commit_with_guarantee are the only two places correctness is actually enforced.

3. The Scalar-Δt Abstraction & the Loosened time_caps

Inside the DP, time is a single scalar dt handed to the entire downstream chain (distribute(c+1, observed_dt, de_budget)). The real system has a window lattice, not one budget. Two deliberate code choices bridge that gap, and understanding them resolves most of the "this looks wrong" reaction.

Choice 1 — time_caps = deadline interval, not min_slack_for_job

# controller._build_dp
time_caps = {(i,j): float(job_d[(i,j)] - job_r[(i,j)]) for (i,j) in proc_jobs[x]}

At the offline-committed state the static min_slack_for_job is ≈ 0 — Phase 5 of the offline solver already ate the static slack. If the DP used it, no action would pass the timing gate in build_job_actions and the online phase would propose nothing. The deadline interval d − r is the loosest structural cap (a job can't occupy more than its own period), so it lets actions through and defers the true test to commit time.

Choice 2 — the chain shares one dt

Every downstream job can, in the DP, draw from the same observed_dt. Physically a far-downstream job cannot use the time slot J* vacated. The model is optimistic on purpose; the dynamic DBF removes whatever is physically impossible.

Is loosening time_caps a bug? No — it is what makes the energy windfall useful. Suppose a window had genuine timing slack the offline phase could not fill because it ran out of energy, not time. Online, an energy windfall (observed_de) arrives. Loosened time_caps + the dynamic DBF let the DP spend that energy on the segment the offline phase couldn't afford. Tightening time_caps back to min_slack_for_job would wrongly forbid this legitimate gain. The cost of loosening is only that the DP sometimes over-plans in windows with no real slack — and those plans are trimmed (see Issue I1).
Why over-planning is caught, not dangerous. If a planned addition lands in a window W containing no early-completer, then demand(W) rises above its offline value. Offline Phase 5 left most windows tight, so demand(W) > |W| and _timing_ok_dynamic rejects it. The only additions that survive are those whose window genuinely gained slack (from a completer) or was genuinely non-tight (energy-blocked offline). Both are correct gains.

4. prune/query Correctness & the Tolerance Asymmetry

Why the O(n²) sort-then-filter prune is correct

prune sorts by (req_t↑, req_e↑, value↓). When entry e is processed, every already-kept entry k has k.req_t ≤ e.req_t, so domination collapses to two axes: k dominates e iff k.req_e ≤ e.req_e and k.value ≥ e.value. That is checked directly. The reverse sweep (drop kept entries this e dominates) only fires on req_t ties, which is exactly when a later entry can dominate an earlier one. The result is the true Pareto frontier, and because query takes the max value over all entries with req ≤ (dt, de), the reconstructed value function is the correct non-decreasing step function.

The tolerance asymmetry — read this carefully

Two different tolerances coexist, and they are not interchangeable:

SiteTestTolEffect
prune (domination)k.value ≥ e.value − 1e-91e-9 An entry whose value is within 1e-9 of a cheaper one is dropped. Can discard a marginally better point. Negligible for these utilities, but it means the frontier is "within-1e-9 optimal," not bit-exact optimal.
query (afford)req_t ≤ dt + 1e-91e-9 An entry needing a hair more than the windfall is still considered affordable. Safe because the real feasibility test is the commit-time DBF, not this.
controller energypool − spent ≥ −1e-61e-6 A thousand-fold looser. A commit can technically overspend the pool by up to 1e-6 energy units. Harmless in magnitude, but note it is not the same slack as the DP's 1e-9.
DBF demanddemand > (t2−t1) + 1e-91e-9 Window feasibility uses the tight 1e-9.
The mix is a latent foot-gun. The DP reasons at 1e-9; the energy guarantee at 1e-6. If you ever scale energies down (so 1e-6 is significant relative to a segment's a_e), the energy guard becomes the loosest link. Standardising on one tolerance, or making it relative, would remove the asymmetry.

5. Why There Is No Double-Spend Across Events

A natural fear: event 1 credits J*'s freed time in window W and spends it; event 2 (another completer overlapping W) credits and spends again — could the same window slack be sold twice?

No. _commit_with_guarantee at event 2 calls _timing_ok_dynamic, which recomputes demand(W) from the current seg_k — already including event 1's additions. So event 2 is checked against the post-event-1 state. The window's remaining room is whatever is actually left. The same holds for energy: the pool is a single running scalar, decremented as it is spent, so the second event sees the reduced pool. Both resources are tracked in live mutable global state, which is precisely why re-checking (not a-priori reasoning per event) is what keeps it sound.

This is the upside of the "rebuild + global re-check" design that Part I noted as a cost: it is also what makes multi-event correctness fall out for free.

6. Complexity: Where the Time Actually Goes

Let, per processor: N = jobs in the EDF chain, A = actions per job (≤ (N_seg+1)·N_frq), F = max Pareto-frontier size of a level.

OperationCostNotes
build_job_actionsO(A)nested k×z loop.
compose (one level)O((A·F) + C²), C = A·F Cartesian product then O(C²) prune. The prune dominates. Corrected 1 Oct 2026: the prune is now an exact O(n log n) Pareto sweep (Fenwick prefix-max over rank-compressed req_e), so a level costs O(C log C) and the Cartesian product itself dominates — C Entry objects get built before anything is pruned.
build_processor_dpO(N · (A·F)²) One precompute answers every suffix/completion point.
queryO(F)linear scan of the level.
distributeO(N + F)one query + pointer walk.
_timing_ok_dynamicO(N_prc · W² · J) W = distinct arrival/deadline instants, J = jobs/proc. Called once per candidate in the greedy trim.
on_completionO(N·(A·F)² + |plan|·DBF) Dominated by the per-event DP rebuild and the repeated DBF in the trim, not by the lookup.
The headline "microseconds per completion" is the lookup (query/distribute), which is genuinely tiny. But the code also rebuilds the processor DP and may run the DBF several times per event. For the testcase this is negligible; at scale the rebuild (Issue I7) and the O(W²·J) DBF (Issue I6) are the two things that grow.

7. Controller Invariants (what is always true between events)

  1. Feasibility: after every on_completion returns, _timing_ok_dynamic() is True and energy_pool ≥ −1e-6.
  2. Energy identity: energy_pool + total_energy() = B + Σδe (cumulative freed energy), per §1.
  3. Monotone baselines: seg_k[(i,j)] only ever increases (assumption A1, enforced because actions start at k_off); a committed freq_idx may move either way.
  4. eff_override only for completed jobs: a key in eff_override means that job has fired its completion event; its value ≤ its committed effective time.
  5. DP freshness — corrected 1 Oct 2026. This used to say dps[x] reflects the post-commit baselines. It does not. Step 5 now reuses the event-scoped table built before the commit, so the resting dps[x] is deliberately one event stale. Nothing in the decision path reads it — every event builds its own scoped DP, and verify_single builds its own too. pos_of is unaffected either way: chain positions depend only on the static (deadline, release, task, job) sort, so they never change.
  6. applied is append-only and equals the full committed decision history.

If you write new code against the controller, these are the contracts to preserve. Breaking #4 (e.g. setting eff_override for a not-yet-run job) would let the time check bet on the future and is the fastest way to introduce an unsound deadline miss.

I1. distribute()'s value could exceed the committed utility (the scalar-Δt gap)

Symptom

dps[x].distribute(...) returned total_value = V, but _commit_with_guarantee kept a subset summing to g < V. Measured at its worst: the DP promised 83.26 and 33.42 survived — ~60% of the plan discarded.

Root cause — two causes were conflated, and only one was real

This entry used to blame the scalar dt: several downstream jobs sharing one tight DBF window, each fitting the scalar budget alone but not together. Instrumenting every trimmed action showed otherwise. Of 15 planned actions, 8 committed and 7 were dropped — all 7 infeasible in isolation. Not one was a shared-window conflict. The split was:

  • (a′) loose per-job cap — 100% of the loss. time_cap = d − r is the loosest structural bound that exists, so the DP priced and ranked additions the DBF would reject outright.
  • (b′) scalar dt shared across the chain — 0% of the loss.

So the fix this entry originally proposed — a per-window requirement vector, the costly state enlargement — targeted a cause worth 0.0000.

What landed (2 Oct 2026). One method, controller._time_caps(): the cap is now each job's real room in its binding DBF window against the dynamic schedule, reusing _window_residuals() already built for the §IX.B demand test. The original objection to tightening (§3: "static min_slack_for_job is ~0, so the DP would propose nothing") does not apply — static slack was never the right measure. The slack this phase exists to spend is the dynamic slack early completion creates, and that is not ~0. Fix (a) — verify_single asserting added ≤ dp_value + tol — had already landed separately.
Measured — paired A/B, identical instances, only the cap differs.
broad mix (180)tight ρ = 0.25–0.5 (100)
utility promised but undeliverable217.7 → 1.4245.3 → 19.0
online wall time−24.6%−15.4%

promised and committed now agree, which matters beyond tidiness: the DP selects by value, so differently-inflated options were distorting the ranking.

The trade, stated plainly. This was not a pure win and was not landed as one. The −1.8% on the broad mix sits in n_tsk — tight windows, caps bite hardest, and the online phase contributes least there. The +4.6% is in the energy-constrained regime this work is about. It was taken for the honest ranking, the speed, and that gain.

Before anyone "improves" this further. The loss is not the cap being too tight: on the worst regressor, 0 of 5 decisions the old code committed would have been forbidden by the new cap. It is path dependence — once the two arms diverge at the first event they follow different trajectories, and the old over-planning plus the graceful k−1, k−2 degradation acted as accidental exploration. The old 60% trim was never pure waste; it was the running cost of a generate-and-test strategy that sometimes explored better. Any further change here should be A/B'd the same way, not reasoned about.

On testcase.py the new caps give +96.33 instead of +33.42, in 2.3 s instead of 7.4 s. That instance has a 10→240 period spread, so d − r was pathologically loose; generated instances (base × 2m, kmax=3) are much milder, which is why the sweep deltas are smaller than that headline.

I4. Cross-event energy reservation is soft (the "two completions" question)

Root cause

Energy is one global pool; time slack is per-processor and perishable. Events are serialised, and the completer's processor asks for energy first, so whoever the loop reaches first spends the pool and the next one gets the remainder. The §IX.B routing cannot arbitrate this: the processors it compares against have no windfall at that instant (dt = 0), so energy buys them nothing, their marginal is ~0, and the first completer almost always wins. In effect the event ordering is the allocation policy, and at a tie the decision falls to _event_order's (job_r, job_d, i, j) — bookkeeping, not physics.

Measured, 100 tight-energy instances. Every instance has cross-processor ties (595 groups). Permuting only within tied-release groups — the most conservative perturbation possible, leaving global release order intact — changed committed utility on 56 of 100:
orderingtotal online addedvs default
worst permutation seen1179.36−16.6%
default (job_r, job_d, i, j)1414.35—
best permutation seen (oracle)1523.87+7.7%
the paper's density tie-break1289.18−8.9%
A 24.4% spread, and the paper's own proposal — "consider the job which has more higher density in the future" — lands below arbitrary ordering (better on 22 instances, worse on 33). It fails for the same reason the old I2 density filter did: density counts a processor's remaining optional utility while ignoring whether it has the time to run any of it, so the pool can go to someone who cannot spend it.
Where the earlier argument flips. The “fix” this entry once proposed — a hard reservation — was withdrawn on 1 Oct because energy is durable and time slack is perishable, so withholding energy from a processor with a live time opportunity to serve one without is strictly worse. That reasoning assumes one processor has a windfall. At a genuine tie both do, both hold perishable slack, and it becomes a real contest — the single case where reservation would be justified. This entry measures what that case is worth.
Decision (2 Oct 2026): keep first-come-first-served, unchanged. The sort key stays (job_r, job_d, i, j); no behaviour changes and no published number moves. The justification is not “the future is unknowable” — at a tie both windfalls are observed and each processor's marginal is queryable from its DP, so that alone would not defend the choice. It is that every candidate rule is myopic (each optimises the instant while blind to what a processor's next windfall would have done with the same energy, which genuinely is unknown), and no tested rule beat arbitrary ordering — the only principled one tried went 8.9% backwards. So the rule that adds no unfounded machinery, and is deterministic and reproducible, is kept.

Recorded in the code, because the key reads as incidental and is not: simulator._event_order now carries the measurement and a do-not-tidy warning. Changing it silently shifts every online result by up to 24%.

Still open, and genuinely so: batching truly simultaneous completions into one joint allocation by live DP marginal — the paper's own “Δe is shared, what if two early completions?”. The +7.7% best-permutation figure is an oracle over arbitrary permutations, not something any rule has been shown to reach, so that upside remains unquantified.

Low

I5. The trailing block in distribute() is unreachable as written

Root cause

The while loop exits when e.nxt is None. The only entry with nxt = None is base_level()'s, which has k = None. Every composed entry is created with an integer nxt = d_idx (in compose). So when the loop exits, e is the base entry (k=None) and s == N; the trailing block's guards (s < N and e.k is not None) are both false. It never appends.

Why it matters

Purely readability: it implies a terminal-job case that the loop already handles, sending a reader hunting for a scenario that cannot occur.

Confirmed by instrumentation, 1 Oct 2026. Over a full hyper-period on testcase.py: 57 distribute() calls, 15 decisions emitted by the while loop, and the trailing block's guards satisfied 0 times — it was never even reached, let alone made to append.
Fix: delete the trailing block and add one line of comment that the loop's last iteration (where e.nxt points at the base level) already emits the final job's decision. Add a unit test asserting distribute emits a decision for the last EDF job when it changes.
Low

I7. Full per-event DP rebuild & single-hyper-period scope

Root cause

Step 5 of on_completion does dps[x] = self._build_dp(x) — a complete recompute of every suffix level for the processor, even though only the jobs at/after the completion changed baseline. And the whole construction covers exactly one hyper-period's job set; there is no carry into the next hyper-period.

Why it matters

It contradicts the "precompute once, then only lookups" ideal (the lookup is O(F) but the rebuild is O(N·(A·F)²)). For a long hyper-period with many completions this is the dominant online cost.

Fix: rebuild only the suffix from the changed position (V_levels[0..c] depend on V_levels[c+1..], so only levels ≤ the touched position need recomposing), or patch incrementally. For periodicity, re-instantiate the controller per hyper-period with carried-over energy_pool.

9. Mental-Model Corrections After Part I

You might still think…Actually…
energy_pool equals B − total_energy() throughout. Only at construction. Afterwards pool = B − total_energy() + Σδe (§1). The two drift apart by exactly the freed energy.
The DP guarantees the committed plan. The DP proposes; the commit-time dynamic DBF + pool guard guarantee. The DP value is an optimistic ceiling (I1).
distribute returns the committed decisions. It returns planned PlannedActions. The controller may trim them to a feasible subset before they become JobDecisions.
Lower frequency saves energy (paper Options 3). Not under α=1, β=0.5 — fmax is energy-optimal, so lowering frequency costs energy. The conversion actions are inert. Superseded: under the current α=0.15, β=1.0 the conversions are live — 6 frequency changes were committed online on testcase.py, all on provably-unstarted jobs. The misconception is now the row's own “Actually” column, not the claim it was correcting.
"Precompute once" = one static table for the whole run. One static table per processor per event; it is rebuilt after each commit (I7). Within one event it is a pure lookup.
A verify_single mismatch is a correctness bug. It means trimming happened (I1). The schedule is still feasible; only the DP-value == banked-value identity broke.
time_cap = the job's real timing slack. At runtime it is the loose deadline interval d − r; the real slack is enforced later by the DBF (§3).

10. Quick Reference

Energy identities (per controller, cumulative over the run)

pool + total_energy() = B + Σδe  •  actual_energy = B − pool = total_energy() − Σδe
invariant: actual_energy ≤ B   (hard)  |  wcet_energy = total_energy() ≤ B + Σδe  (may exceed B)

Tolerances in play

1e-9prune domination, query affordability, DBF window demand
1e-6controller energy-pool guard (≥ −1e-6), verify_single equality, is_feasible pool check

The two enforcement points (everything else is advisory)

Correctness lives in exactly two lines of _commit_with_guarantee: _timing_ok_dynamic() (no deadline miss) and energy_pool − spent ≥ −1e-6 (no budget overrun, realised). The DP, arbitration, and time_caps are all heuristics feeding these two gates.
